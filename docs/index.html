How many batteries can the arbitrage market take?

Model of day-ahead arbitrage as more batteries enter. The fleet charges and discharges in the day's cheapest and most expensive hours, and that moves the price itself. At what fleet size does revenue per MW fall, and how much does the spread shrink? It depends on how sensitive the price is. That is estimated from historical load, wind, solar and price (see further down), or you can set it yourself.12, 13

Empirical basis: how much does the price move per GW?

The estimate compares the hourly price with residual load (consumption minus wind and solar) over the last 24 months, accounting for month and hour of day. Wind and weather make residual load vary on its own, and the price follows. That is the same thing a battery does when it charges or discharges.14, 15

Definitions and notes

Day-ahead price
The power exchange's price for the next day, set the day before, per bidding zone and in euro per megawatt-hour (EUR/MWh). It is not the end-customer price, and it includes neither taxes, grid fees nor the retailer's margin. Intraday and balancing prices are not included.
Bidding zone
Sweden is divided into four (SE1–SE4). Finland, Denmark (DK1 and DK2) and Germany (here DE-LU, together with Luxembourg) are shown for comparison.
Average price
The average day-ahead price over the period, where all hours carry equal weight.
Price volatility
Here measured as the average daily spread, the day's highest minus lowest hourly price, over the latest twelve months. Larger swings within the day mean more room for flexibility such as batteries and demand response.
Consumption
Actual total load per bidding zone as reported to ENTSO-E, summed over twelve months and shown in terawatt-hours (TWh).
Arrows (Overview)
Compare the latest 12 months with the 12 months before. An arrow pointing up or down means a change of more than 1 % (for solar capture rate, more than 1 percentage point). Smaller changes are shown as flat. The arrows show direction only, not whether a change is good or bad.
Capture price
The average price a technology has received when it produced, that is, the price weighted by production hour by hour.
Capture rate
Capture price divided by the average price, in percent. 100 % means the technology received a price equal to the average, and lower values mean production falls in hours with lower prices than average.
Rolling 12 months
The same calculation over the latest twelve months in one go, weighted by production. It is not an average of twelve monthly ratios. The Duck curve tab also offers rolling 6 months for daytime and night-time prices. The dashed line then shows the same period a year earlier (for rolling 12 months that means months 13–24 back), so that you can see the change compared with the year before.
Daily spread
The difference between the day's highest and lowest hourly price, in EUR/MWh. Shown as an average over the days of the month, or rolling over twelve months.
Share of negative prices
Hours with a negative price divided by all hours in the period, in percent. "Latest 12 mo" is the twelve most recent complete months and "Months 13–24" the twelve months before that.
Daytime and night-time price
The average of the hourly prices at 10:00–14:00 and 00:00–04:00 Swedish time respectively, that is, the hours starting at 10, 11, 12 and 13, and at 00, 01, 02 and 03.
Solar park + BESS
A calculation for a fictional 1 MWp solar park with a battery (BESS). The battery shifts production each day from the cheapest to the most expensive hours. "1 h", "2 h" and "4 h" state how long the battery can deliver at full power.
Share via battery and cycles
Share via battery is the part of the solar park's production that is charged into the battery. Cycles is the energy discharged during the period divided by the battery's capacity, that is, the number of full cycles.
Arbitrage depth
How large a battery fleet the day-ahead market can absorb before arbitrage stops paying. The saturation point is the fleet size at which revenue per MW has fallen to a given share of the first MW's revenue.
Price impact
How many euro per megawatt-hour the price moves when a 1 GW fleet charges (price rises) or discharges (price falls) at full power. It is the model's most important and most uncertain assumption.
Duck curve
The shape of the price over the day. When a lot of solar power is produced in the middle of the day the price is pushed down, while the evening becomes more expensive when the sun disappears. The curve then resembles a duck seen from the side.
  1. Capture rate. Calculated as the sum of price times production divided by the sum of production, and then divided by the average price. Wind is onshore plus offshore wind. Production is ENTSO-E's actual aggregated generation per production type.
  2. The spread is hourly. It is calculated on hourly averages of the price, per day in Swedish time, and not on quarter-hour prices. Short price peaks and troughs are therefore smoothed out, and the figures are lower than if calculated at 15-minute level. Days with fewer than 22 hours of data are not counted.
  3. Spread is not revenue. The measure shows the theoretical difference between the day's highest and lowest price. Anyone who wants to earn on the spread, for example with a battery, is affected by efficiency, number of cycles per day, power and energy limits, grid fees, and differences between day-ahead, intraday and reserve markets. The outcome is therefore lower.
  4. Solar and wind: coverage. The quality of reporting to ENTSO-E differs between countries and over time. Solar production in Sweden in particular is incomplete, because much solar power is small-scale or behind the meter and is not fully reported. The figures show reported production and not necessarily the full actual production. Months where production data is missing for more than 20 % of the hours are shown as empty.
  5. Resolution. Day-ahead has had 15-minute resolution since October 2025 (hourly prices before that). Everything is aggregated here to hourly averages so that older and newer data are comparable. A negative quarter-hour within an hour is therefore not visible if the hourly average is above zero.
  6. Which months are shown. The current month is not shown, nor are months where more than 10 % of price data is missing. Production data is revised afterwards by the grid operators, so earlier months may change slightly at the next update.
  7. Time of day. All times and days refer to Swedish time (CET/CEST). "10–14" means the hours 10, 11, 12 and 13, and "00–04" means the hours 00, 01, 02 and 03. A daytime or night-time price is only shown for months where all four hours are available.
  8. Daily profile. The profile is an average over all days in the period, weighted by the number of days per month. Summer is April–September and winter October–March. The average hides the variation between individual days, for example sunny and cloudy days.
  9. The solar park. Production is calculated with PVGIS for 1 MWp of crystalline silicon in Halmstad (56.7° N, 12.9° E), 30° tilt, facing due south and 14 % system losses. The profile comes from a historical weather year and is placed on the same dates over the last twelve months. It therefore does not follow the actual weather. In reality the price often falls when it is sunny, so the capture rate for a real park is likely somewhat lower than here. Halmstad is in SE4, and the other zones are shown as hypothetical comparisons with the same production profile.
  10. The battery model. The battery's power equals the park's peak power (1 MW per MWp). Duration is 1, 2 or 4 hours and round-trip efficiency 88 %. The battery is charged only from the solar park, never from the grid, and is empty at the end of the day. For each day the charging and discharging hours that give the highest revenue are chosen, with knowledge of all the day's day-ahead prices in advance. Production is not curtailed, and losses in the battery count as sold energy that does not materialise.
  11. Capture price with battery is revenue divided by the solar park's production, including battery losses. Capture rate is capture price divided by the zone's average price over the same period. The calculation does not include grid fees, balancing costs, intraday trading, ancillary services, degradation or investment costs and should be seen as an illustration of the price value in the day-ahead market.
  12. The arbitrage depth model. The fleet consists of identical batteries with power N and duration 1, 2 or 4 hours, 88 % round-trip efficiency, and charges only from the grid. The batteries are price takers with perfect foresight of the day's day-ahead prices, and are empty at the end of the day. The price in that hour is shifted linearly with the fleet's net flow (price impact in euro per MWh and GW). Because the batteries push the price up when they charge and down when they discharge, revenue per MW falls as the fleet grows. The calculation is based on hourly prices for every fourth day over the last 12 months, scaled up to a year.
  13. Limitations. The result depends entirely on the price impact. The model has a single price level per zone and does not account for trade with neighbouring zones that dilutes the effect, for supply and demand changing over time, or for batteries also selling in intraday and ancillary service markets. It does not include grid fees, operations, degradation or investment costs. It shows an upper limit for what day-ahead arbitrage yields, and not what an individual installation will earn.
  14. How price impact is estimated. For each zone a regression of hourly price on residual load (consumption minus wind and solar, in GW) is run over the last 24 months, with fixed effects for month (which captures fuel prices and hydrology) and for hour of day split into weekdays and weekends. Prices are capped at the 1st–99th percentile so that single spikes do not dominate. Standard errors are cluster-robust by week. Because weather co-varies between zones and the zones are priced together, four variants are run: own residual load only, with Germany as a control, with all other zones as controls (the main estimate, including the five Norwegian zones), and the same on only the latest 12 months. The bands in the charts span the three controlled variants, and the variant without controls is shown only for comparison because it comes out too high.
  15. What the estimate does not show. It measures how the price has moved given the variations in residual load that have actually occurred, often a few hundred MW up to about a GW in a Swedish zone. A battery fleet of several GW lies outside that range, and the line is then an extrapolation. When the batteries fill up the interconnectors to neighbouring zones, the zones decouple and the price becomes more sensitive, so real saturation may come faster than a straight line suggests. The relationship is also affected by things that are not included, for example flows, nuclear outages and hydrology.
  16. Consumption. ENTSO-E's reported actual total load per bidding zone, aggregated to hourly values and summed over twelve months. Months with fewer than 90 % of the hours reported are left out, so a rolling 12-month figure requires twelve complete months. The grid operators may revise the data afterwards.
